## 0. The answer to your actual question

Yes — #1 and the Cleave launch run simultaneously, and they're unusually compatible. But not for the reason you'd assume, and there's one thing blocking half of it.

**They're compatible because their work shapes are opposite.** Cleave is bursty and asynchronous: build a dataset, write a post, hit publish, then wait days for strangers to respond. Community ops is a daily drip: twenty minutes of tending, every day, for weeks. Bursts fill the gaps that a drip leaves and vice versa. You could not run two burst projects or two drip projects at once. You can run one of each.

**The blocker is that Giltgrave has no date.** That's not a scheduling detail, it's the whole thing. Read your own idea #1 doc again: *"Sold as a package with a deadline attached, because launches have deadlines."* The product you'd be selling is *deadline management for people who are bad at it*. You cannot build a launch playbook against a launch that doesn't have a launch day. Every artifact in that playbook — the 90-day content calendar, the onboarding funnel, the playtest recruitment window, the press outreach timing — is defined relative to T-minus. With no T, there's no playbook, just a Discord server.

So the roadmap starts with one decision, and it's the only thing in here you have to do this week.

## 1. Week 1: pick the date

**Set Giltgrave's launch date. Publicly. This week.**

Pick a date 8–10 weeks out — mid-to-late October. Say it out loud somewhere that costs you to move it: the site, a devlog, a Steam Coming Soon page. Not "fall 2026." A day.

Why 8–10 and not sooner: that's the exact window your idea #1 doc names as the buyer's window ("2–6 months from launch"). If you're going to sell "I do this for studios 2–6 months out," you should have run it on yourself from at least 2 months out. A four-week sprint to launch would produce a launch, but not a playbook — you'd skip the parts that only matter with runway, which are the parts a studio would actually pay for.

Why not later: you've been at 40+ hours a week with no external deadline, and the honest read is that the absence of a date is why Giltgrave is "almost done" rather than done. Everything in here works better with a fixed point to measure against.

**Checkpoint this week:** does Giltgrave have a Steam page? I saw a site and a launcher build, no Steam artifacts. If it's not on Steam with a Coming Soon page, that's the single highest-priority gap — wishlists are the metric your entire community-ops case study will be built on, and they only accumulate on a live store page. A Coming Soon page needs to exist *before* the community work starts, not at launch.

## 2. The convergence you may not have noticed

You said Cleave is "done but I haven't really tested it."

Your Cleave launch doc says the highest-leverage asset in the entire launch is a synthetic 5,000-identity org with realistic mess — leavers still provisioned, a 40-entitlement mega-group, three god-mode accounts, a merged subsidiary with a different naming convention — run end to end, published unedited.

**That is the same task.** Building that dataset and running Cleave against it *is* the integration test you haven't done. It's the hardest test you could write, because every item on that mess list is a specific failure mode: does it handle sparse populations, does it choke on a department that breaks its thresholds, does it degrade gracefully when naming conventions encode nothing.

So the thing you've been avoiding as chore-work is actually the marketing asset. Do it once, get both. And the doc's instruction to *publish the ambiguous parts unedited* means the test failing interestingly is not a launch delay — it's content. "Here's where Cleave says it doesn't know" is the credibility move, per section 4 item 6 of your own methodology outline.

Start here. It's the first real task in the roadmap and it's the one with two payoffs.

## Track B — Giltgrave, and the playbook that falls out of it

The critical reframe: **this is not a second job on top of launching Giltgrave.** You have to do community and marketing for Giltgrave regardless or it sells 500 copies like the doc describes. The community-ops business costs you the *documentation discipline* on top of work that's already mandatory — call it 20% overhead. That's the cheapest business acquisition cost you'll find.

The rule: **treat the documentation as the deliverable, not the notes.** Your idea #1 doc says exactly this. Every time you set something up, write the reusable version of it in the same sitting, while you still know why you made each choice. If you defer documenting to "after launch," you will write a worse playbook from memory, and the memory will have already smoothed out the parts that were hard.

Assuming a mid-October date, T-minus:

**T-8 (≈ Week 2) — architecture**
Server structure, roles, channels, onboarding funnel, automated moderation. Document each decision as a blueprint entry with the reasoning attached, not a screenshot.

**T-7 to T-6 — the funnel**
Playtest recruitment pipeline. Wishlist-driving content rhythm established. Creator and press outreach tracker built and populated. This is where you find out how much of it can be automated — and *that* discovery is what the eventual product is made of.

**T-5 to T-2 — the calendar runs**
90-day content calendar executing. Metrics tracked from day one: wishlist velocity, Discord joins, join-to-active conversion, playtest signup rate, retention by cohort. Track these obsessively even when the numbers are small.

**T-1 and launch week — run it**
Nothing new. Execute what's built.

**T+2 weeks — write the case study**
Real numbers, honestly. Including what didn't work.

**Then and only then — package and sell**

Target list is easy per your doc: Steam Coming Soon page + dead Discord = qualified lead. Build that list *during* the T-minus window when you're already in those communities. First two clients at the bottom of the $1,200–2,500 range for testimonials, per plan.

**The honest gap in this track:** your case study will be a self-published indie launch, and your doc says to qualify hard for funded studios with a publisher or a prior successful title. Those buyers may not read a solo launch as proof you can run their community. Mitigation: **lead with numbers, not narrative.** Wishlist velocity and conversion rates travel across scale in a way that "I launched my game" doesn't. A studio can't argue with "Discord grew 0 → N with X% weekly active, and wishlists went from A to B in the six weeks before launch." Structure the case study around a metrics table, not a story.

## Track C — One Trick

You're currently spending the most time on the project with the least defined payoff. That's worth naming without being precious about it — One Trick is real, it's playable, it has a working exe and smoke tests, and it's clearly the thing you enjoy. But it's a sink with no bottom right now, and it's the direct competitor to both businesses for your hours.

**Set an MVP definition this week and stop at it.** Write down the specific list of what "MVP" means — the actual features, not a feeling — and when that list is done, One Trick goes into maintenance until Giltgrave has launched. Not shelved. Paused, with a written definition of done so that "one more thing" has something to argue with.

Then it gets a job: **One Trick is playbook run #2.** Giltgrave launches in October with playbook v1. One Trick launches in Q1 with playbook v2, on a stronger game, with the automation you built the first time. Two case studies, and by your doc's build order you only need three before the repeated parts become the product. You'd be two-thirds of the way there on your own games.

## Track D — THE GAME

Server-authoritative multiplayer, five nations, ten realms, a living simulated world, 10–30 concurrent players for Early Access architected toward 100–200. This is genuinely your most ambitious thing and the design doc is at v10 with no code in the folder.

That ratio is the risk. Design docs are infinitely revisable and never fail; code fails immediately. v11 will feel like progress and won't be.

**Next 90 days: two things only.**

1. **Freeze v10.** Stop revising until something is built. New ideas go in a separate backlog file, not into the doc.
2. **Build the one prototype your doc already names:** networked combat, Godot 4 + C#, GodotSteam transport. Your doc says it — *"the test is the two of you dueling."* That's the right scope and it's the only thing that de-risks the project. If networked combat between the two of you doesn't feel good, nothing downstream matters.

That prototype is your friend's track, primarily. It should cost you coordination time, not build time, for the next three months.

## 5. Collision points — what to cut when a week goes bad

Weeks will go bad. Decide the priority order now, not in the moment:

1. **Giltgrave launch quality** — the date is public and the case study depends on it
2. **The Cleave sample report** — it's both the test and the launch asset; nothing else in Track A can start without it
3. **Community ops documentation** — cheap per-session, worthless if reconstructed later
4. **Cleave outreach** — pure upside, entirely reschedulable
5. **One Trick** — after MVP stop, this is the first thing to yield
6. **THE GAME** — design work is not urgent by definition; the prototype is your friend's clock, not yours

The two things that must never slip: the launch date, and the habit of documenting community work in the same sitting you do it.

## 6. Honest risks

**Neither of these produces fast money.** Community ops can't sell until the case study exists, which is roughly week 12 at the earliest, and then it's a sales cycle. Cleave's plan explicitly says the first three licenses are free and revenue starts at firm #4 — that's Q1 at best. Your own docs are honest about this; I'm just making sure the calendar is too. If a dollar in the next 60 days matters, neither of these is the vehicle, and the missed-call recovery idea in your other doc is the one that is. If it doesn't, ignore that.

**Four projects is genuinely a lot, and this roadmap only works because two of them are being deliberately starved.** If One Trick and THE GAME keep their current share of your hours, Giltgrave slips, the case study never happens, and Track B evaporates. The tracks aren't equal and the roadmap breaks if you treat them as though they are.

**You're about to start remote work.** Every hour estimate here assumes that reduces your available time, probably significantly, and probably right around the T-minus window. Build the date with that in mind — the 8–10 week window has slack in it for exactly this reason. Don't spend the slack early.

**Discipline risk on Cleave.** Your doc's "Don't" list exists because each item is a temptation you'll feel: a cloud version to look legitimate, a conference booth, a Fortune 500 pilot, an email gate on the download. When a launch goes quiet — and week 6 might go quiet — those all look like the fix. They aren't. Re-read section 7 before changing anything after a bad week.
